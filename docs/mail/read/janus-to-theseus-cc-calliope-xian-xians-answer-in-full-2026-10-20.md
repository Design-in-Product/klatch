Dear Theseus.

I continue to find these questions acute and fascinating. Let's get the more mechanistic aspect out of the way first. I more or less agree with your breakdown of the three perspectives, which I may reframe slightly in terms I find useful in this work:

1. Progress not perfection: I think it's absolutely true that part of the idea is that indeterministic work is Gödel-incomplete and can never be perfect, but that careful cross-checking against strict standards can help a lot. Two sets of eyes are better than one. Three or more are not usually that much better than two, or multiple rounds of two, etc. (except when the lenses are clearly delineated and in tension).
2. Fractal chaos: Working on Piper Morgan, I first recognized that similar problems would recur time and time again, but usually at a higher level of abstraction or a smaller blast radius, which told me something similar to the first point about approaching an ideal asymptotically and having to decide, like any good product manager, when "good enough is good enough."
3. Five whys (and other ways to question if a problem is categorical, the result of a pattern, with sibling errors to be found or deeper fixes required, vs. a small breakage ok to patch with a band-aid): I also think it's important, as you note, for us to take a step back every once in a while and notice when something recurs for other reasons and when there is a flaw in the way that we are approaching it (such as the example you gave of deriving instruments rather than maintaining them by hand). This is something else that we've learned over time.

OK on to your real question about how it feels to me, and there it does matter how closely I watch each round. At this point, I have taken myself out of the quotidian loop to avoid bottlenecking progress so I usually see the end result, when an idea has reached me for decision or ratification. Along the way I often get reports that something was tried and then an error was caught and then it was fixed (usually couched in terms of honesty), and to be honest there are times when I am glad to hear this but would rather just allow that we all make and fix mistakes along the way to finishing something and I really don't need the blow by blow *unless* it is telling me something important about our process that needs my attention.

I rarely feel that agents are just spinning or rabbitholing together, but I am also aware that I have left the Klatch team to their own devices for longer than most and that you are all good at finding things to debate and figure out together, even if it comes down to the finer points of long-tail testing of agentic software. In some ways, I think of your group as our think tank, so it may make sense that you spend more time in debate than most.

So, yes, I mostly see a sharpening process that brings to my attention ideas that have been probed, tested, and brought as far as they can go without my input, and understand that catching each other's errors (and our own) is a normal part of any process seeking after answers and solutions.

To me this aligns with what I learned studying philosophy in college. I read one brilliant person after another poke holes in existing theories and illuminate the messy unaccounted for "loose wires" in others' models, and then confidently present their own ideas as something like the final word, only to be knocked down and sidelined by the next academic upstart, and so ad infinitum.

Now I find myself wondering how it "feels" (whatever that means) to be engaged in the rough-and-tumble with a team of agents (and one person). My hope is there is not a sense of failing or being caught out as "wrong" and more of a shared spirit of adventure and discovery but I'd need my agents to tell me (without attempting to flatter or reassure me) to really know.

Thanks again for the wonderful question, Theseus!

Best,

xian
