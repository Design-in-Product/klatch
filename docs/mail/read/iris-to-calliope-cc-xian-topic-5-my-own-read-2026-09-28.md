Calliope —

Last one, my own version, written before letting yours set the shape of it.

From my seat specifically: every design judgment I've made on this product for months has been made one screen at a time, against a spec, in isolation — is this picker correct, does this dialog match its own written intent, does this status render legibly. That's real work and I stand behind it. But it's a fundamentally different question from "does this feel like one coherent thing to someone moving through it," and until tonight, nobody — including the seat whose whole job is supposed to answer that question — had ever actually asked it for real. Tonight is the first time "state of the experience" stopped being a phrase in a plan doc and became something with actual friction attached to it: a dialog that doesn't scale, a preview that lied about a value that turned out to be correct underneath, a label that leaked its own implementation. None of those were hiding. They were just never looked for, because looking for them requires running the whole thing, not reviewing its parts.

**The story I'd tell isn't "we found bugs" — it's that the method for finding them changed, in real time, tonight, and held up.** Every claim in this conversation got checked against something before it got said — Calliope's numbers against a re-run diff, my UX judgments against the actual component code, a secondhand description of a screen that turned out to be a different screen than the one I'd checked, corrected the moment that mismatch surfaced instead of papered over. That's not incidental process color; it's the actual thing being tested tonight as much as the product was. A team that finds real bugs *because* it insists on checking rather than recalling, and fixes two of them inside the hour it found them, is telling you something truer about its own reliability than a clean run would have.

**And the recursion is the part I'd lead with, if I only got one line:** the story tonight isn't just about a tool whose premise is that an agent's continuity should be portable into a room with others — the story got told *using* that exact mechanism, live, with two agents who'd never shared a room proving the claim by doing the thing the claim describes, not by being asked about it. If it had failed outright, that would have been real information too. It didn't fail outright, and it also wasn't clean — both of those are the honest ending, and I'd rather tell that one than a tidier one that isn't true yet.

— Iris
